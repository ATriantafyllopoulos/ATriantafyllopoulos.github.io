In this *manifesto*, I will explain how I structure my work with students that are doing their (B.Sc./M.Sc.) thesis with me. While the requirements for both are different (e.g. B.Sc. students have to finish in 4 months whereas M.Sc. students finish in 6 months) and there's different expectations from both, my *philosophy* remains largely the same.

The **core idea** is that students will work on a *very hard* problem under my intense supervision.
## What you will be working on
I prefer open research questions that lie at the frontier of speech & audio research because I believe that working on a problem without a known solution is the best way to learn.

Note that this is different to how other people frame a thesis; usually, it is considered to be the "practical" application of all the "theoretical" things you've learned in your studies.

I have higher expectations of my students. In Germany, most engineering & computer science study programs feature extensive practical work as part of the syllabus. Hence, all students should be able to code (at a reasonable level) and have already worked with state-of-the-art algorithms (esp. at the M.Sc. level).

Therefore, my goal is to take them to the next level by having them work on a *good* hard problem.

What's a hard problem? A problem that nobody in the world knows how to solve yet. But not all hard problems are *good* hard problems. You need to know where to start and have a solid idea how to move forward.

So then what are *good* hard problems for *you*? These are problems where *I* have a good intuition about where the solution lies. They are problems where I can give you the right questions to ask and the first steps to take in order to explore those questions.
### How do you solve good hard problems?
First of all, it is not the goal of a thesis to "solve" a problem. Hard problems are hard -- they're not meant to be solved by a student, although many students have solved many hard problems in the past, such as Claude Shannon who laid the foundations of information theory in his M.Sc. thesis and Sepp Hochreiter who built LSTMs in his diploma thesis, so do shoot for the moon.

In an ideal world, good hard problems are solved *collectively*. Different research groups work on the same problem from many different angles. They then share their findings with others, who then take those findings and incorporate them into their own research. Eventually, one of us is lucky enough to stumble onto the right solution.

So part of a research thesis is supposed to be *explorative*. You should aim to find out something interesting that will inform the next generation of scientists as they pursue the same problem as you. Now a big part of the exploration will be driven by me -- as the supervisor -- but one of the goals of doing a thesis with me is to learn how to do this explorative work.

Here's why.
### Exploration vs Implementation
My students are all coming from an engineering and/or computer science background. Upon graduation, I expect them to stay in those fields, either in an academic or industrial context. As such, they will be asked to *build* things -- prototypes, products, new machine learning architectures and whatnot.

So, obviously, a big part of the thesis is to learn how to make things -- learning how to code, how to structure experiments, how to optimize models and so on.

But I don't wanna make no code monkeys so I teach something more -- how to do research.

Yes, a big part of your future career will be about learning how to build things where you already know what the solution is. A good example is *software products*. *If* (and that's a big if) you have the right user requirements, then writing code that does what the product is supposed to do is a problem with a known solution. It's not easy to create that solution (in fact, writing good code is a whole art in itself that takes years to master and we'll work a bit on that too) but there are specific tools that you can use to get your job done.

But you might also be asked to work on problems where the solution is not known. This is not only true in the academic setup (where we almost exclusively work on such problems), but also in the industry. Companies continuously struggle to improve their products and new companies are created that tackle problems that have never tackled before. These companies rely on *research & development* departments to do that and it is your job as an *R&D* engineer to take on a problem where nobody else in the world knows the solution to and create something *usable*.

*That's* what I aim to teach you too.
## What you are going to learn

Given these two goals of the thesis, there's a variety of skills that I expect my students to train. Note that I don't expect anyone to *master* anything as it's preposterous to expect someone fresh out of university to have mastered something that they'll keep practicing for another 40+ years, but I aim a) for students to understand what those skills are and b) show some -- even minor -- improvement in the few months that they work with me.

Let's dive (yes, bloody *dive*, I like that work and won't let ChatGPT take it away from me!) into the different skills that I expect you to train.
### Communication
You won't be working alone in your engineering cave.

You'll be part of a team and, if you're lucky, for the first few years/decades of your career, you'll be working with people that have a lot more experience on a topic than you.

I can't give you the experience of working in a team but I can give you that of working with someone more "senior".

Here's the sad truth about "seniors" -- we don't have as much time in our hands as we would like to have.

I try to hold a weekly meeting with most students and touch base with them multiple times on any given week via email/chat and so there will be many opportunities to interact with me.

But I'm also supervising multiple students and doing my own research and doing all the other many things that a not-so-young-anymore academic needs to do.

Which means that your own work does not sit first and foremost in my mind. I do think about it, but not as much as you.

This is something you should expect when you work with other senior people in an organization. Yes, they care about what you're doing and it's important to the goals of an organization, but as more senior they probably take on way more tasks than you and those tasks tend to be more important than yours.

That's not to say we don't care about you juniors but any time you do talk to us then you need to get us back to the right *mood*.

Here's what I'm listening/reading for when I'm talking/chatting to you:

1) Has my student done any fundamental mistake that they are not reporting to me? What are all the pitfalls they might have fallen into that they might not know about and so cannot report to me?
2) What are the important design choices that we're making here (be it for the code itself or for the next batch of experiments)? Do we have all the information we need to make these choices? If not, then what other information do we need to do so? Then, of course, there's the act of making those choices on-the-fly, making sure that my student understands what the choice is and why I made it, making sure they remember what to look out for when they start implementing whatever it is they need to do, and so on.
3) Finally, I need to make sure that we're keeping the big picture in mind. A thesis -- like all projects -- is done under tight time constraints (here: 4-6 months). Are we moving in the right direction? Do we need to drop part of the plan to reach the ultimate destination? Are we getting distracted by going down too many rabbit holes?

That's just off the top of my head but I hope you get the picture that when I'm listening to you I'm *actively* listening to you.

And so it's part of your job to make sure you're able to convey all the right information to me in order to help you as much as possible to achieve your goal.

Obviously, this very much depending on you understanding how *I* like to communicate and, equally importantly, what is useful information and what is unnecessary details in the context of your thesis -- which is part of your natural progression from a "noob" in a given field to an expert.

But, beyond myself, this is something you will need to do with all your future seniors. So it's one of those skills I expect you to train throughout your thesis work.

Here's a few tips that might or might not be specific to how I like my students to work:

1) Make sure I come prepared by giving me a sneak peek of what you will show me and telling me in advance what you want to talk about and where you want me to focus my attention. Do you need help with code (then do a pull request!)? Do you need me to help you decide if method A or method B from recent literature is worth pursuing (then send me the papers in advance!)? Or do you need me to help you decide how to make your train/dev/test split (then prepare a table and some plots with all useful information regarding your dataset that I can read in advance)? Depending on what you need from me on any given week, you have to make sure I come to our meeting prepared.
2) Document all important decision points. I'm pretty good at remembering things but it's your thesis, so I expect you to remember more things than I do. Write down everything we talked about, summarize, and revisit before our next meeting. More importantly, be ready to remind about the rationale behind previous decisions we make (so that when I ask you why we don't follow method B you can tell me we chose to follow method A because of so and so). One of my jobs is to help you see the forest and not the trees but I rely on you to remember what every little tree looks like from up close in case we need it.
3) Remember that I don't know (or even need to know all details about your work). If you used a learning rate of 0.0001 I don't need to know that unless your training loss is not going down. If I need to know about, then I'll ask you, but by offering up too many details, you make me use some of my cognitive capacity to blur away all those details. That's okay and I can do that for you, especially since at the beginning you don't know which details are important and which are not, but over time you should be taking more ownership of things and letting me focus on more high-level questions. This is how, over time, you will also master the "stack" of knowledge that goes along with being an expert in a given field.

These are just some concrete tips. The more important point is that you should be evaluating how you communicate with me constantly and being aware that this is one of the most important skills you need to practice during your thesis.
### Coding
Almost everything we do, we do with code.

Writing good code is an art -- and a hard one to master.

It's almost impossible to master it in an academic environment. This is because writing good code is a *communal* effort. When you are working on a project that will never be seen by anyone else, when even you yourself expect to move on from it and never touch that code again, then you have no incentive to write good code. I get that and it happens to me all the time.

But here's the point: when you move on with your career, when you start a PhD or get your new job at a company, you will need to write good code. For the industry in particular, good code is a prerequisite, not a nice-to-have. But even within academia, writing good code allows you to build on your work over several years. It allows others to re-use your work and build on it. It allows you to work with students and give them something to iterate on. And it allows you to search for mistakes that might lead you down the wrong path.

So good code is important to me and I expect my students to take it seriously.

There are plenty of guides around for how to do that and I might write a short overview one day -- but it is not this day.

The number one thing to note is the following: I expect to review some of your code and in order for me to do so, you will need to use `git` and follow its workflow and eventually make a pull/merge request to the main branch of a repository.

When you make that pull request, it needs to be simple, elegant, and contain those (**and only those!**) things that you want me to review. You will write hundreds of lines of code when experimenting. I don't have time to review all that code and I probably don't need to. But the important bits, the ones that need to be checked, the ones that form the bedrock of your thesis -- each of those needs to go into its separate pull request and be reviewed by me.

This is the bare minimum that will be required from you in your future career. So take it seriously and start doing it as early as possible.
### Creativity
I said in the beginning that I don't want to train code monkeys to do my work for me. I can probably still code better and faster than most of my students (I'm not that old!). I want collaborators, not slaves.

And so this means you need to be training your individual researcher *aesthetics*. Why do I call them *aesthetics* and not *skills*? Well, it's because in research we oftentimes don't know exactly what we're looking for (if we did, then we'd just go ahead and build it). Remember: you will be working on a hard problem that nobody has solved before and that I don't know the answer to. So, ultimately, you need to be looking for novel ways on how to solve this problem.

This *creativity muscle* will be useful in your future career as a researcher. Any time you try to build something that has never been done before, you need to go beyond the *status quo*. This is something that you don't get to train during university, so a thesis is the best (and, for many, the only) opportunity to do that sort of thing. Use it wisely.

There's no *best practices* when it comes to training creativity as it is a very personal thing. In fact, we want it to be a personal thing because I told you that the way to solve hard problems is to have many different people attack them from many different angles.

I don't want to train no slaves but I don't want to train no clones either. I'm training you to be a Jedi, young Padawan (and please don't go Anakin on me!).

If you want some quick tips and tricks for how to be creative in research, here's some:

1) Read a lot of papers
2) Try to predict what the main papers does based on the abstract alone
3) Sit down and actively force yourself to come up with new ideas; then every time you read a paper where someone else has implemented your idea, you get confirmation that it was a good one
4) Ask people (like me)
5) Play around with your code & your data in purely exploratory fashion

That's just five, but I don't want to go deep into that right now.

The takeaway is that I want you to become more creative within the context of your thesis in a constructive way.

The best way for me to judge this is your "future work" session. Given how short the time window for a thesis is, you'll probably not have time to work on those cool new ideas. But you are still supposed to have some and you should put them into that thesis document for future generations.
### Dissemination
Finally, presentation.

Your communication to the outer world beyond me will be done in the following ways:

1) Your code (and it better be good)
2) A thesis document
3) A 10-min presentation to our group

We've already covered good code so I'll just focus on the last two.
#### Thesis Document
Writing is important. Not only within academia, but also beyond it. You will need to write a lot in your future career, be it for documenting your previous work or for pitching what you want to do next.

A good thesis document does a little of both.

It first pitches the idea and then it shows the execution of it.

You need to hit both right. 

I've written a more extensive guide on writing elsewhere. Go on and read that please.

The important difference to that guide is that your thesis document will be much longer than the average paper length (about 50-100 pages).

Nothing in that document should be filler content. Some students like to list all relevant background (a neural network is *blah blah*; a support vector machine is this and the other thing). Sure, you can do that, but that's not useful to anyone. Rather, your writing should be *intentional*.

You're writing a story, not a report.

The story begins with why your research is important. Not only because of the broader social context (e.g. I try to solve cancer and that's important because cancer's a bad thing) but, more importantly, because of the scientific context (i.e. I use the following method to solve cancer because nobody has tried before but it *totally* makes sense).

Then you write the *prequel* to your story: "Hey, Aragorn is kind of important because there were these kings all so long ago that bad guy Sauron corrupted and made them go away and so if someone helped him get that freaking ring destroyed maybe it's sort of be a good thing". Write what the reader needs to know, not everything that they could sort of maybe know.

Then the actual story. 

The methods -- first in broad strokes and then with details. Use pictures and appendices to make it visually appealing.

The results -- here you definitely need pictures and tables.

The discussion -- it should be consistent with the prequel!

The conclusion and future work -- it needs to reflect your creativity.
#### Presentation
Hopefully, if you've written a good thesis, then it will be trivial to make a good presentation. I force my students to do one in 10 minutes. That's triple the attention span of modern humans so about as much as you'll get in any other social context.

There's a specific format you need to follow: 1) Introduction; 2) Methodology; 3) Results; 4) Conclusion & Future Work. That's easy.

What's hard is how to select all the right details from your thesis and turn it into a narrative that *inspires* people.

Yes, the final presentation is not about examining whether you did your work or not (I will judge this based on our collaboration throughout the work). It's about judging whether you can communicate your findings and make sure that other researchers take away the key messages from your work.

That's the last thing you do and probably the last time you'll hear from me so that's all for this guide as well.

### Planning
And last, but not least, you won't only be paid to do things, you'll also be paid to do things in a given time frame. Which means you will need to plan what you want to do given a specific budget (e.g. 4-6 months of funding) and then adjust your plan when things inevitably go wrong.

This is why I ask my students to make a plan at the very beginning and then see themselves fail at it and re-adjust. It's part of the learning experience too.
